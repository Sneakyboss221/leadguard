\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether two appealing mechanisms, quality-gated lead attention and evidential abstention, help an ECG
classifier survive lead failure once its training already includes such failures. In a controlled ablation on
PTB-XL, they did not. Training on simulated failures delivered the robustness, \gainDropKTen{} macro-AUROC for
the same ResNet with ten leads missing; quality gating added a provable invariance to dead leads but no
measurable accuracy; and the evidential uncertainty stayed flat while accuracy fell from \aucLgEdlKZero{} to
\aucLgEdlKTen.

That last result is the one we would put in front of anyone deploying ECG models: a network can be as sure of two
leads as of twelve, and an uncertainty score that behaves well on clean data offers no protection unless it is
tested under the failures it is meant to catch. Until it is, an explicit check of the signal is the safer alarm.

The natural next steps follow from our limitations: real rather than simulated artifacts, including intermittent
contact and swapped electrodes; a second dataset; more seeds for the ablation; and uncertainty methods that are
explicitly asked to respond to missing information, for instance an evidential objective tied to the remaining
signal, or ensembles. We release the code, trained models, result files and an interactive demonstration so that
each of these questions can be tested against the same baselines.
